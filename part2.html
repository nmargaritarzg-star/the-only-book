Part II · Middle

The Tower

974 words · about 5 min read

Orren told them the story while the candle burned down.

He had been nineteen when the drum chose him, a baker’s son who liked climbing roofs and arguing with crows. He had climbed the Tower full of questions — hundreds of them, so many he could hardly breathe. And he had started to read.

“The first pages are wonderful,” he said. “You learn why the sky is blue, and it is more beautiful than you expected. You learn how bees talk to each other. You learn the names of your great-great-grandparents and what they dreamed about. Every page is a door opening.”

“And then?” said Kai.

“And then the doors keep opening, and you notice something. Each answer closes a question. That is what answers do. A question is a little empty room inside you, and the answer fills it. By the middle of the Book, I had very few empty rooms left. By the end…” He opened his old hands, as if letting something go. “By the end, I knew everything. I knew what you would say before you said it. I knew what the weather would be on my hundredth birthday. I knew how every story ended, so I never wanted to hear one. I have not been surprised in fifty years, Kai. I have not been curious. I have not wondered.”

The room was very quiet. Above the glass dome, a star slid down the sky. Orren did not look up. Of course, Mira thought. He already knew it would fall.

“Then why does anyone read it?” she asked.

“Because the city needs answers,” said Orren. “And because nobody asks what it costs.”

Kai was staring at the Book. Mira could see his jaw working.

“My sister’s farm,” he said. “The well. The whole south is drying up. People are leaving. If I read the Book, I’ll know where the water went. I’ll know how to bring it back.”

“Yes,” said Orren. “You will.”

“Then I have to.” Kai stood and went to the lectern. He put his hand on the earth-coloured cover, and Mira saw his fingers tremble. “It’s just questions. I’ll trade my questions for water. That’s fair.”

“Kai —“ Mira began.

But he had already opened it.

She had expected light, or music, or some terrible wind. There was nothing. Kai simply began to read, his lips moving slightly, his eyes going left to right, left to right. The candle flickered. Orren closed his silver eyes.

Mira sat on the cold floor with her notebook on her knees and watched her friend.

At first he was all excitement. “Mira — did you know the moon is moving away from us? A little every year. And the stars we see — some of them aren’t even there anymore, it’s old light —“ He laughed. He turned a page. “Ink is black because of iron and oak galls, that’s the old kind, and soot, and — oh, that’s so simple.” He laughed again, softer.

By midnight he had stopped telling her things.

By the second hour, he had stopped laughing.

By dawn, when the glass dome turned grey, then pink, then gold, Kai was halfway through the Book, and his face was calm. Not peaceful. Calm the way a pond is calm when nothing lives in it. When the sun came through the dome and touched the lectern, he didn’t look up. He already knew it would.

“Kai,” Mira said. “Kai, what colour is music?”

It was one of the questions from her notebook. Last year he had spent a whole afternoon arguing that trumpets were orange.

Kai turned a page. “The question is incorrectly formed,” he said in a flat, polite voice. “Music has no colour. Some people experience synaesthesia. It is explained on page four hundred and twelve.”

Mira felt something break very quietly inside her chest.

She stood up so fast her notebook fell open on the floor. Orren opened his eyes and looked at it — at the pages of her small, crowded handwriting, question after question after question.

“You knew this would happen,” she said to him. “You knew and you let him.”

“I knew,” Orren agreed. “I also know what you will do now. But knowing is not the same as doing, child. I cannot do it for you. That is the other thing the Book took from me.”

“Then tell me,” Mira said. “Tell me how to stop it.”

Orren was silent for a long moment. Then he lifted one thin finger and pointed — not at the Book, but at the empty shelves curving away down the walls of the Tower.

“Why do you think,” he said slowly, as if every word cost him, “someone built nine hundred and ninety-nine steps of shelves for a single book?”

Mira looked at the shelves. Then at the Book. Then at her notebook, lying open on the floor.

“For other books,” she said. “Someone thought there would be other books.”

“There were,” said Orren. “Once. Before the city decided one was enough. The people who kept writing went out past the walls, to the salt plains. The city calls that place the Margins, because it is the edge of everything.” He coughed, a dry paper sound. “And Mira — the Book contains everything. Everything that is. Ask yourself what it cannot contain.”

“What is the answer?”

For the first time, Orren Hale’s silver eyes looked almost amused.

“I am not going to tell you,” he said. “You would never forgive me.”

Mira picked up her notebook. She looked at Kai, bent over the Book with his calm, empty face, turning page after page. Three hundred pages left, maybe less.

“How long until he finishes?”

“Tomorrow at sunset,” said Orren.

Mira buttoned all eleven pockets of her coat and ran down nine hundred and ninety-nine steps.
