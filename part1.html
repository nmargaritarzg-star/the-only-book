Part I · Beginning

The Lottery

1148 words · about 6 min read

In Hollowmere there was only one book, and everybody agreed that one was enough.

It lived at the top of the Tower of the Single Book, on a lectern of black stone, and it contained everything. The weight of the moon. The recipe for bread. The name of every star and every fish. Why people cry at weddings. What would happen tomorrow, and the day after that, and on the last day of the world. Whoever read it from the first page to the last would know all of it, and would never have to wonder about anything again.

Only one person at a time was allowed to read it. That person was called the Keeper, and the Keeper answered the city’s questions.

Below the Tower, along Lantern Street, stood the Answer Halls. Their windows were tall and their doors were always open, and above each door hung an iron lamp shaped like an open book. If you needed to know something — how to treat a fever, when to plant barley, whether your neighbour’s dog was really a fox — you went to an Answer Hall and wrote your question on a grey card. The clerks carried the cards up to the Tower once a week. A week later, the answers came down.

Mira Vell was a clerk at the Third Answer Hall, and she was very bad at it.

Not because she was slow. She was the fastest copyist on Lantern Street. Her fingers were permanently blue with ink, and she could copy a Keeper’s answer in a hand so neat that customers sometimes framed it. She was bad at it because she read the questions.

Clerks were not supposed to read the questions. They were supposed to sort them. But Mira read every card, and some of them stayed in her head like splinters. Why does the sea taste of salt but the rain doesn’t? Will my son remember me? What colour is music? She kept a small notebook in the inside pocket of her grey wool coat — the coat had eleven pockets, which was ten more than a clerk needed — and in that notebook she wrote down the questions she liked best. Not the answers. Just the questions.

Writing was not exactly forbidden in Hollowmere. You could write a shopping list. You could write a question on a grey card. But writing a book — writing anything long, anything that tried to explain or imagine — was considered strange, like lighting a candle at noon. Why would you? Everything had already been written.

Mira had never told anyone about the notebook. On its cover, in very small letters, she had written: The Book of Questions.

On the morning of the Lottery, the bells of the Tower rang fifty times.

Once every fifty years, the old Keeper grew too tired to keep, and a new Reader was chosen from the young people of the city. The names went into a glass drum in the square. The drum was turned by the oldest woman in Hollowmere. A child pulled out a single slip of paper. The chosen one climbed the Tower, read the Book, and never came down. Keepers stayed at the top and answered.

Mira stood in the crowd with her hands in her pockets. Next to her stood Kai Dorran, who was tall and sunburnt and wore a blue jacket so patched it looked like a map. He was the son of a well-digger from the dry farms south of the city, and he had been her friend since they were seven, when he had asked her why ink was black, and she had said she didn’t know, and they had both been delighted.

“If it’s me,” Kai said, “I’ll find out why my sister’s well went dry. I’ll find out where the water went. The Book knows.”

“The Book knows everything,” Mira said. It was what people said. She didn’t know why it made her feel cold.

The drum turned. The child — a small girl with a cold — reached in, pulled out a slip and handed it to the Mayor, who unfolded it and read, in a voice like a door closing:

“Kai Dorran.”

The square cheered. Kai went white under his sunburn, then red, and then he laughed, the way he always did when he was frightened. People slapped his back. Someone put a garland of paper flowers around his neck. Mira found that she was holding his sleeve so hard her knuckles hurt.

“There’s a tradition,” Kai said quietly, when the crowd had pushed them to the steps of the Tower. “The Reader can take one companion up the stairs. To turn the pages, they say. Just for the first night.” He looked down at her. “Will you come?”

“Why me?”

“Because you’ll ask questions,” he said. “And I’ll want someone to ask them to, before I know all the answers.”

So that evening, with the lamps of Lantern Street glowing behind them like a row of open books, Mira Vell and Kai Dorran began to climb.

The Tower had nine hundred and ninety-nine steps. Its walls were lined with shelves, floor to ceiling, all the way up — and every single shelf was empty. Not dusty, not broken. Simply, perfectly empty, as if they had been built for books that nobody had ever needed to write.

“Why build shelves,” Mira whispered, “if there’s only one book?”

Kai shrugged, breathing hard. “Ask the Keeper.”

Near the top, the stairs narrowed and the air smelled of old paper and candle smoke. A door of pale wood stood half open. Behind it, in a round room under a glass dome full of stars, a very old man sat beside a black stone lectern. On the lectern lay the Book.

It was not as big as Mira had imagined. It was the size of a loaf of bread, bound in leather the colour of dry earth. It looked like something you could carry under one arm.

The old man turned his head. His eyes were strange — pale, almost silver, the colour of a page held up to the light.

“Kai Dorran,” he said. “And Mira Vell, who keeps a notebook in her fourth pocket.”

Mira’s hand flew to her coat.

“Don’t worry,” said the Keeper. “I knew you would come. I have known it for fifty years.” He smiled, but the smile did not reach his silver eyes. “My name is Orren Hale. Sit down. Before you read, there is something you should know about the Book. Nobody has ever asked me about it, so I have never said it.”

“What is it?” Mira asked, before she could stop herself.

Orren Hale looked at her for a long time.

“That,” he said, “is the first question anyone has asked me in fifty years that I was glad to hear.”
